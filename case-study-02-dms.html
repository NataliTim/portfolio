Dealer Management System Modernization

Improving usability, consistency and maintainability while preserving the workflows customers already relied on.

SaaS · Cloud Legacy modernization UX standards Interaction patterns
roleManager of Product Design
scopeDealer Management System
focusIncremental modernization

The Dealer Management System had evolved over several decades and supported deeply established workflows used by experienced dealership teams every day.

The challenge was not simply to modernize the interface. Any significant change to familiar interaction patterns could slow users down, increase training needs, and create resistance.

The business needed a way to improve usability, consistency, and maintainability while preserving the workflows customers already relied on.

The product had accumulated inconsistencies over time as different screens and features evolved independently. The key design questions were:

01How can we improve clarity without disrupting established user behavior?
02Which patterns should remain familiar, and which inconsistencies should be standardized?
03How can we improve information hierarchy, labeling, navigation, and field placement across a large legacy application?
04How can new AI-assisted design and development workflows make modernization of a large legacy product more scalable?

The goal was not to redesign everything from scratch, but to identify the highest-value improvements and introduce them systematically.

05

Use New Technology to Scale Modernization

Modern AI-assisted design and development tools made it possible to approach legacy-product modernization differently.

Design standards, layout rules, and reusable patterns could be translated into structured guidance and automation, reducing the amount of manual work required to modernize a large number of legacy screens.

Instead of redesigning hundreds of screens individually, the approach began shifting toward a scalable system for identifying, validating, and applying improvements.

Customers have worked in this application for over a decade. Speed comes from muscle memory, so every proposed change had to earn its place: it had to make the product clearer, not just newer.

standards

Heuristics, information architecture, accessibility

Screens were measured against usability heuristics, information-architecture principles and accessibility standards, and brought in line with the design system rather than restyled screen by screen.

interviews

Essential changes only, confirmed by users

User interviews were run throughout so that what shipped was essential. Where a change added value only on paper, the familiar pattern stayed — the existing day-to-day process was never disturbed for its own sake.

onboarding

Clarity that shortens training

Consistent labeling, grouping and action placement make the product self-explanatory, so new staff are trained and onboarded on one set of patterns instead of a different convention per screen.
